Does White win more often?

Z-Test Set-up: Let p = the proportion of games won by White, and alpha (significance level) = 0.05.

Set up the Null Hypothesis: White and Black have an equal chance of winning; p = 0.5

Set up the Alternative Hypothesis: White has a higher chance of winning than Black; p > 0.5

Assume that the set of games is randomly sampled, each game is independent, and the games have a normal approximation distribution.

04 / Game length

Do openings lead to different game lengths?

We compare the ten most common openings (excluding "Other") to game lengths by using an ANOVA test.

One way ANOVA Test Set-up: Let μ = the population mean game length for a opening move, and alpha (significance level) = 0.05.

Set up the Null Hypothesis: H0: μ1 = μ2 = ... = μ10; the 10 most popular openings have the same population mean game length.

Set up the Alternative Hypothesis: At least one of the 10 most popular openings has a different population mean game length.

Assume that each game is independent, the games have a normal approximation distribution, and variance is constant.

{{ plot('qq-original', 'Ten normal QQ-plots of original game lengths, one for each of the most common named opening families.', 'Figure 4.1: QQ-plots before the square-root transformation.' ) }}

Looking at the QQ-plots, we see that points curve away from the line of best fit near the ends, which suggest that the data is right skewed. Thus, we cannot assume normality, and need to perform a square-root transformation.

{{ plot('qq-transformed', 'Ten normal QQ-plots of square-root-transformed game lengths, one for each opening family.', 'Figure 4.2: QQ-plots after the square-root transformation.') }}

That's better! Now, we can assume normality for a residual test.

{{ plot('residuals', 'Scatter plot of transformed game-length residuals against opening-family means, with a zero reference line.', 'Figure 4.3: Residuals vs. group means after transformation.') }}

The residual plots show no distinct cone shape, so we can still assume constant variance.

Finally, we can show the spread of game lengths by opening family via a box-plot.

{{ plot('game-length', 'Box plots of square-root-transformed game lengths for the ten most common named opening families, with outliers.', 'Figure 4.4: Distribution of the square-root of turns by opening family.' ) }}

Key Takeaway: Our p-value ends up being 5.3007E-39 < alpha = 0.05. Therefore, we reject the Null Hypothesis. Conclusively, there is statistically significant evidence that at least one of the ten most common openings has a different population mean game length.

And looking at the distributions of each opening family, some contain large outliers, which could contribute to differences in the group means. Otherwise, medians and interquartile ranges are relatively similar across openings.

05 / Conclusion

In Conclusion...

In this collection of Lichess games, players used a wide variety of openings, with Sicilian Defense the most common named opening umbrella. White won slightly more often than Black among decisive games, but win rates varied across opening families. The game-length test also found evidence that at least one opening family's mean differs on the square-root-transformed scale.

Our findings do not prove that playing a specific opening, or playing the first move, can lead to a better game outcome. Hoever, our findings still show how color, opening, and game length can help describe patterns in chess.

Future Work

If we were to continue our research, there are multiple aspects to expand upon, including:

  • Game comparisons across player ratings, time controls, and rated vs. casual play.
  • Study of draws and more specific opening variations, including those grouped into "Other".
  • Use of confidence intervals and follow-up comparisons between openings, to identify which groups differ.
  • Examination of independent samples from other platforms (i.e. Chess.com).
